Chinese-led researchers at the BESIII collaboration presented the strongest evidence yet for the existence of a glueball, specifically identifying the X(2370) particle as a long-predicted composite made entirely of gluons, at the International Conference on High Energy Physics in Natal, Brazil. The discovery has been hailed as a major triumph in particle physics, with discussions of a potential Nobel Prize. This discovery provides the strongest experimental validation of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a cornerstone of the Standard Model, by confirming the existence of glueballs — particles made purely of force-carrying gluons without any quarks. It represents a significant milestone in understanding the strong nuclear force and could reshape how physicists approach fundamental particle research. Glueballs are extremely difficult to identify in particle accelerators because they mix with ordinary meson states, making the X(2370) evidence particularly significant after 15 years of research. The particle consists solely of gluons — massless elementary particles that mediate the strong interaction between quarks — without any valence quarks.

**Background**: Gluons are massless elementary particles that mediate the strong interaction, binding quarks together to form protons, neutrons, and other hadrons. Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the theory within the Standard Model that describes this strong force. Glueballs are a natural prediction of QCD — composite particles made entirely of gluons rather than quarks — representing a unique form of matter where pure force manifests as a particle.

Digital preservation efforts like the Internet Archive and Perma.cc aim to combat this by archiving web content.
