## [Formalizing Fermat's Last Theorem](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formalizing-fermats-last-theorem) ⭐️ 9.0/10

Anthropic has successfully formalized Fermat's Last Theorem using AI-assisted Lean, producing 13 million lines of Lean code and 29,500 intermediate theorems in just under two weeks. A team of agents completed the proof consuming about six billion output tokens from an internal research model comparable to Claude. This achievement demonstrates that large swaths of advanced mathematics can now be mechanically verified, which may help catch errors in the common body of mathematical proofs and reduce the burden of refereeing new work. It represents a major milestone for both AI capabilities and the formal mathematics community. The proof follows the Darmon–Diamond–Taylor exposition from 1995 of the Wiles–Taylor–Wiles argument, developing Fontaine theory and enough of Mazur's work on the Eisenstein ideal. At API rates, the computation would have cost approximately $300,000.

hackernews · jlebar · Sep 4, 18:42 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49568506)

**Background**: Lean is an open-source proof assistant and functional programming language based on the Calculus of Inductive Constructions, designed to bridge the gap between interactive and automated theorem proving. Formal verification is the process of mathematically checking that a system satisfies given properties using formal methods. AI-assisted theorem proving in 2026 operates primarily through Lean 4, which has the largest open-source mathematics library (mathlib4) with over 1.5 million theorems.

## [Actively exploited sandbox RCE in all Chromium versions](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/cve-2026-85046) ⭐️ 8.0/10

CVE-2026-85046 is an actively exploited sandbox escape vulnerability affecting all Chromium versions, with Google paying a $1,000 bounty to the researcher who ethically reported it. The vulnerability is already being exploited in the wild despite the disclosure. This is a high-severity security issue with broad impact since all Chromium-based browsers are affected, and active exploitation means users are at immediate risk of remote code execution. A successful sandbox escape could allow attackers to compromise the entire system, not just the browser. The vulnerability enables remote code execution by escaping the Chromium sandbox, which normally isolates renderer processes from the host system. Google's $1,000 bounty for ethical disclosure contrasts sharply with the potential real-world exploit value of an actively exploited zero-day.

hackernews · negura · Sep 4, 21:52 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49570669)

**Background**: Chromium's sandbox architecture isolates renderer processes using OS-specific features so that code executing in the browser cannot harm the underlying system. The sandbox is a critical security boundary: if an attacker can escape it through a vulnerability, they may achieve full system compromise. Chromium uses techniques like seccomp-bpf on Linux and site isolation to strengthen this boundary, but sandbox escapes remain one of the most dangerous classes of browser vulnerabilities.

Mullvad is discontinuing its public encrypted DNS service and will instead provide financial support to Quad9, citing the specialized nature of operating a privacy-focused DNS and Quad9's leadership in the field. This is significant because DNS is a critical privacy infrastructure component, and the shift from a major VPN provider operating its own DNS to sponsoring another service raises questions about centralization in privacy tools. It also reflects broader trends in how privacy-focused services are being consolidated. Mullvad considers running a privacy-focused public DNS a highly specialized undertaking and believes Quad9 is the undisputed leader in this space. Users who relied on Mullvad's DNS will need to switch to Quad9 or other alternatives.

hackernews · mywacaday · Sep 4, 18:50 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49568579)

**Background**: DNS (Domain Name System) translates domain names like google.com into IP addresses that computers use to communicate. Traditionally, DNS queries are sent in plaintext, making them vulnerable to eavesdropping and manipulation. Encrypted DNS protocols like DoH (DNS over HTTPS) and DoT (DNS over TLS) protect these queries by encrypting them. Privacy-focused DNS services like Quad9 and Mullvad's former service offer additional protections by not logging user queries and blocking malicious domains.
